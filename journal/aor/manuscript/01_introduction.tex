\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Financial research is cumulative. Candidate signals, transformations,
execution rules, and risk controls enter a strategy library after development
and validation, where they remain available for operation, comparison, and
reuse. As the library grows, so do its storage and computing footprint, its
validation and monitoring queue, its documentation and governance obligations,
and the human attention required to understand and maintain it. Retention is
therefore not merely a data-management decision. It is an operations-research
problem about allocating scarce research and governance resources across a
maintained portfolio of strategies.

The apparent redundancy of a strategy depends on which role is being assessed.
For current operation, a strategy is redundant if deleting it leaves every
regime-dependent operating opportunity unchanged. For future research, the
same strategy may be the only carrier of a signal, transformation, execution
component, or risk-control module that can be reused in a later project. A
strategy can consequently be dominated at every current belief and still be
generatively indispensable. Pruning only the current payoff envelope can
preserve what the library can operate now while silently changing what it can
help create later.

We represent these roles by two source-library objects. The operating frontier
\(F_L\) records the best current operating value attainable from a source
library \(L\) at each declared belief. The generative closure \(C_L\) records
the modules available to the modeled strategy-generation process after its
closure rules have been applied. Under the paper's finite factorization and
detectability assumptions, the pair \((F_L,C_L)\) is the library component of
the productive state: replacing \(L\) by a sublibrary with the same frontier
and closure leaves the modeled operating opportunities and generative menus
unchanged. The filtered belief and any active-project variables remain part of
the full controlled state; the reduction is not a claim that the two library
objects replace the entire stochastic state.

This semantic reduction defines \emph{innovation-safe compression}. Given a
source library, additive retention burdens, and a mandatory inactive strategy,
we seek a minimum-burden source sublibrary whose frontier and closure equal
those of the source exactly. Equality is source-relative and exact: it is not a
prediction criterion, a statistical tolerance, or a claim of equivalence in
unobserved markets. The source itself is feasible, but choosing the least-cost
feasible representation is a global combinatorial optimization problem.
Sequential deletion can certify that every accepted step is safe, yet an
inclusion-wise irreducible endpoint need not have minimum burden.

The identity-closure subclass exposes the combinatorial structure sharply. In
that subclass, the requirements are tagged to keep frontier-attainment rows
distinct from module-carrier rows, including frontier ties and requirements
already met by the inactive strategy. A sublibrary is safe if and only if its
strategies cover every remaining tagged requirement. This equivalence reduces
the semantic retention problem to a weighted covering formulation; it does not
claim set cover itself as a new problem. The restricted decision problem is
NP-complete, and the broader compression model is NP-hard because it contains
that subclass. The reduction therefore supplies both a modeling bridge to the
classical covering literature \citep{Karp1972,Chvatal1979} and a reason not to
expect local redundancy checks alone to solve global resource allocation.

We address this boundary with a portfolio of algorithms rather than a single
method. Complete enumeration provides a small-library oracle.
Requirement-mask dynamic programming is exact when the tagged requirement
dimension is manageable. Proven preprocessing rules force unique carriers,
remove safely dominated or empty variables, merge duplicate coverage and
requirement patterns with explicit reconstruction maps, and can sometimes
resolve an instance completely. An auditable mixed-integer formulation scales
the covering model while retaining solver diagnostics and an independent exact
postcheck of the returned library. Weighted greedy construction inherits its
classical covering guarantee under identity closure, and reverse deletion can
reduce its burden without weakening that guarantee. Deterministic and
multi-start deletion heuristics provide certified feasible endpoints, but
their feasibility certificates are not certificates of optimality.

The computational evidence is designed to reveal where these distinctions
matter. A prospectively registered benchmark separates small instances with
exact cross-method comparisons, structured instances with controlled and
realized coverage features, and adversarial mechanism families. Its audited
results identify settings in which inexpensive methods attain exact or
certified reference burdens and settings in which method choice changes the
retained burden. Failed and resource-limited runs remain part of the reporting
denominators. The benchmark is synthetic evidence about the registered
generators, not a population claim about financial research libraries.

The application evidence comes from separate retrospective terminal and
annual walk-forward financial library audits. They compare safe algorithms
under predeclared burden schedules and recheck mandatory retention,
frontier equality, closure equality, and burden exactly. The audits quantify
resource savings and show why equal-cardinality conclusions need not settle a
heterogeneous-burden retention decision. Financial quantities from held-out
periods do not enter weights, preprocessing, algorithm choice, warm starts,
pruning order, or optimization; they are read only as postdecision opportunity
diagnostics. Terminal and annual units remain separate. These exercises do not
claim causality, forecasting skill, alpha, or deployable performance.

\subsection{Research questions}

The analysis is organized around four questions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Under the declared finite partial-information process,
which library summary is sufficient to preserve both current operating
opportunities and future strategy-generation capabilities?

\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How does exact minimum-burden preservation become a
covering problem under identity closure, what computational barrier follows,
and how far can a certified local-deletion rule be from the global optimum?

\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Which exact, approximation-guaranteed, mixed-integer, and
certified heuristic methods can exploit this structure while preserving exact
feasibility and an auditable reconstruction to original strategy identities?

\item[\textbf{RQ4.}] Across the registered synthetic benchmark and the
retrospective financial audits, when do inexpensive safe methods match a
certified reference, and when does exact global optimization change retention
burden or selected identities?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

The paper makes four principal contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Dynamic semantic reduction.} We identify the operating frontier
and generative closure as the sufficient library summary under the declared
finite process and formulate innovation-safe compression as exact,
source-relative preservation of both objects. This separates operational
redundancy from generative redundancy without replacing the belief and project
components of the controlled state.

\item \emph{Complexity and worst-case theory.} For identity closure, we prove
the tagged-cover equivalence and NP-completeness of a restricted safe-compression
decision problem. A parameterized family gives the exact burden ratio of the
heaviest-safe-first endpoint to the global optimum and shows that this named
deletion rule has no constant approximation guarantee. The result does not
apply to every deletion order.

\item \emph{Algorithms and preprocessing.} We provide a common exact instance
representation; optimum-preserving preprocessing with reconstruction;
complete enumeration; exact requirement-mask dynamic programming; an auditable
mixed-integer workflow; weighted greedy and reverse deletion; and deterministic
and randomized certified deletion methods. Exact postchecks establish returned
feasibility and burden. They do not turn a solver status into a formal or
exhaustive proof of global optimality.

\item \emph{Registered computational and financial evidence.} The locked and
audited benchmark compares exact, solver-based, and heuristic methods across
registered structural and adversarial families. The terminal and annual
walk-forward audits then quantify burden savings and selection sensitivity
under predeclared weights while preserving the licensed-data and
held-out-information boundaries. The two evidence layers remain distinct.
\end{enumerate}

The theoretical, computational, solver, and financial evidence levels are not
interchangeable. Universal claims rest on their stated human proofs and, where
the theorem ledger says so, narrower kernel-checked declarations. Exact finite
computation establishes facts about specified fixtures or instances. Solver
diagnostics document a numerical global-search process, whereas independent
exact rechecks certify the feasibility and burden of the returned sublibrary.
Registered synthetic results describe the locked generators, and retrospective
financial results describe the audited source instances. The algorithmic
guarantees in this article apply to identity closure unless a result explicitly
states otherwise; we do not claim a covering algorithm for arbitrary
nonidentity closure.
